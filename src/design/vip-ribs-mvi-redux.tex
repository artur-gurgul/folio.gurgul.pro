\sheettitle{VIP · RIBs · MVI · Redux · MV — the architecture map}{design · folio}

\oneliner{Every UI architecture answers three questions — \textbf{who owns the state},
\textbf{which way data moves}, \textbf{where side effects run} — and these patterns differ only in
their answers.}

\vspace{2pt}
{\small\setlength{\tabcolsep}{3pt}\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.08}
\noindent\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{14mm}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{40mm}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{56mm}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{44mm}@{}}
\toprule
 & \textbf{owns the state} & \textbf{data moves} & \textbf{side effects run in} \\
\midrule
\textbf{VIP} & the scene's Interactor & one way round: VC $\to$ Interactor $\to$ Presenter $\to$ VC & Workers the Interactor calls \\
\textbf{RIBs} & each RIB's Interactor & down a tree of business logic; listeners report up & the Interactor, as Rx subscriptions \\
\textbf{MVI} & one immutable State per screen & View $\to$ \emph{you} $\to$ Intent $\to$ Model $\to$ View & drivers, outside the pure program \\
\textbf{Redux} & one Store for the whole app & dispatch $\to$ reducer $\to$ every subscriber & middleware (thunks) — never a reducer \\
\textbf{TCA} & one Store, scoped per feature & actions into reducers composed into one & the Effects a reducer returns \\
\textbf{MV} & \texttt{@Observable} models & the view reads the model and calls it & the model's own methods \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}}

\section{VIP — Clean Swift}

  \node[gy] (wo) at (6.85,-0.3) {Worker \textnormal{\small API, DB}};
  \draw[flow] (in) -- (wo);
  \node[note] at (4.0,-0.3) {turns one way only};

Uncle Bob's Clean Architecture, cut down to \textbf{one screen}. Because the cycle turns only one
way, each role is tested alone against a hand-written \textbf{spy of the next role} — Clean
Swift's pitch is that you ``don't need to learn any testing or mocking frameworks''. The price is
ceremony: \textbf{6 files per scene}.
\begin{itemize}
  \trap{``VIP is VIPER renamed.'' VIPER's Presenter sits \emph{between} View and Interactor,
        both ways, and Entities stay in the Interactor; VIP is a one-way cycle.}
\end{itemize}

\section{MVI — Cycle.js}

  \draw[flow] (mm.north) |- node[lbl, left, pos=0.3]{state} (mv.west);

\textbf{You are the other half of the loop.} Cycle.js treats the interaction as a dialogue: ``both
human and computer would be functions with inputs and outputs'' — the screen is your input, your
events are its input. The program stays \textbf{pure}; \emph{drivers} do all the I/O. ``A MVI tuple
is a UI program'' (Staltz, 2015).
\begin{itemize}
  \trap{A one-off message kept in state shows again on every re-render, until the view
        reports it shown and the state clears it.}
\end{itemize}

% ── MV and TCA ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{TCA and MV}
\textbf{TCA} is Redux with the effects brought inside: a reducer \emph{returns} its effects as
values, so the TestStore makes you prove every step — ``each step of the way we need to prove that
state changed how we expect'', and an effect that feeds data back must be asserted too.
\textbf{MV} drops the ViewModel: SwiftUI tracks the properties \texttt{body} reads, one by one, so a
plain \texttt{@Observable} model can sit right behind the view. The \texttt{Observable} protocol
alone observes nothing; the macro does.

\textbf{``Business logic drives the app, not the view tree.''} LoggedIn has no view at all: it
only decides which child is attached, OffGame or Game, and the screen follows. RIBs came out of
Uber's rider-app rewrite, when a few hundred engineers shared one MVC app and its
\texttt{RequestViewController} had grown from 300 to 3,000+ lines. The same tree runs on iOS
and Android.
\begin{itemize}
  \trap{The Router is not ``just navigation'': it attaches child RIBs from Interactor
        output; screens follow.}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{State is a fold over actions.} Reducers are named after \texttt{Array.reduce()}: they
``reduce a set of actions (over time) into a single state''.

\noindent\begin{tikzpicture}[sheet, x=1mm, y=1mm]
  % the label sits UNDER the arc's lowest point: on the arc (first build) it ran into the text
  \path[use as bounding box] (0,-12.5) rectangle (80,5);
  \foreach \s/\x in {0/4, 1/26, 2/48, 3/70} \node[st] (s\s) at (\x,0) {$s_\s$};
  \foreach \a/\f/\t in {1/0/1, 2/1/2, 3/2/3}
    \draw[flow] (s\f) -- node[lbl, above]{$a_\a$} (s\t);
  \draw[hot, dashed] (s3.south) to[bend left=16] (s0.south);
  \node[lbl, text=sheetOrange] at (37,-10.5) {time travel: replay $a_1 \ldots a_k$ from $s_0$};
\end{tikzpicture}

That is why they must be \textbf{pure}: Redux was written in mid-2015 to show
\textbf{time-travel debugging} — replay the actions up to any point and you have the state there.
\textbf{Flux} (Facebook, 2014) began with a chat that told users they had a new message when they
had none; its cure was one rule: only a dispatched action may change a store.

\begin{itemize}
  \trap{Every subscriber hears every change unless it \texttt{select}s a slice; an open
        dropdown does not belong in the store.}
\end{itemize}
